Sports Injury Prevention: The Complete Guide for Athletes, Parents and Coaches

By PRO ATHLETE Team · Designed by health professionals · 10 min read
Key takeaway: Most sports injuries aren't bad luck. A short neuromuscular warm-up done 2 to 3 times a week (balance, landing, agility and strength) is one of the most effective ways to keep athletes healthy, on the court and on the field.

Whether you're an athlete, a parent, a coach or an athletic director, this guide explains why injuries happen, which ones are the most common, what the research says actually works, and how to put it into practice this season.

1. Why injury prevention matters

An injury is never just a few games missed. For a young athlete, it can mean losing a spot on the team, losing confidence, falling behind in development, and sometimes dropping out of sport altogether. For families, it means medical appointments and lost time. For schools and clubs, it means weaker rosters and more liability concerns.

The good news: a large share of sports injuries can be prevented with simple, structured exercises, done regularly.

2. The most common sports injuries

In jumping, cutting and pivoting sports like basketball and soccer, the same injuries come up again and again. Knowing them is the first step to preventing them.

Ankle sprains

The most common injury in basketball, and very frequent in soccer. Often caused by a poor landing or a sudden change of direction, and likely to come back if not properly rehabilitated.

3. What actually works: neuromuscular training

Stretching alone and a few laps around the gym don't prevent injuries. What the research consistently supports is neuromuscular training: exercises that teach the body to land, decelerate, change direction and stay stable under fatigue.

The best-known example is the FIFA 11+, a 20-minute warm-up developed for soccer. In a meta-analysis, teams using it had significantly fewer injuries (Thorborg et al., 2017). Similar programs exist for basketball and other sports, and they all share the same building blocks.

Two conditions make the difference between a program that works and one that doesn't:

  • Frequency: 2 to 3 times per week, all season long, not only in pre-season.
  • Quality: exercises done with good technique and progressed over time. A warm-up that never changes stops challenging athletes.

4. The 5 components of an effective warm-up

Whatever the sport, a good injury prevention warm-up includes these five elements, in 10 to 20 minutes:

  1. Gradual cardio activation: light running with direction changes to raise body temperature.
  2. Balance and proprioception: single-leg exercises, with or without a ball, to improve ankle and knee control.
  3. Strength: lunges, squats, Nordic hamstrings, core work. The muscles that protect the joints.
  4. Jumping and landing: learning to land softly, knees aligned over the feet, on one and two legs.
  5. Agility and sport-specific speed: cutting, deceleration and sprints that mimic game situations.

Frequently asked questions

At what age should athletes start injury prevention exercises?

As early as they start playing organized sport. Neuromuscular warm-ups (balance, landing, agility and strength exercises) are safe for children and teens when they are adapted to their level, and building good movement habits early is one of the best long-term investments an athlete can make.

How often should an injury prevention program be done?

Aim for 2 to 3 sessions per week. Research shows that the benefits depend on doing the program consistently throughout the season, not just in pre-season.

How long does an injury prevention warm-up take?

Most evidence-based warm-ups take 10 to 20 minutes and replace a regular warm-up, so they don't add time to practice.

Does an injury prevention program replace physiotherapy?

No. Prevention programs are for healthy athletes who want to lower their risk of injury. If an athlete is injured or has persistent pain, they should be assessed by a health professional, who will guide their rehabilitation and return to play.

Do injury prevention programs work for sports other than soccer and basketball?

Yes. The principles (balance, landing control, strength and agility) apply to most jumping, cutting and pivoting sports, such as volleyball, handball, hockey, football and pickleball.
